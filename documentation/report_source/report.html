1. Introduction

Students lose things on a college campus every day: identity cards dropped near the gate, calculators left in classrooms, wallets forgotten in the canteen, earphones, keys and books. At VJTI these belongings are usually traced through word of mouth, notices on boards, the security desk or messages in class groups. These channels are scattered, short-lived and hard to search, so many items are never returned even when the person who found them wants to help.

LostLink is a mobile application that gives the campus one shared place for lost and found belongings. A student who loses something posts a lost report; a student who finds something posts a found report, optionally with a photograph. Other students browse and search these reports. When a student recognises a found item as their own, they submit an ownership claim describing details that only the real owner would know, and the student who found the item reviews the claim and approves or rejects it. An administrator moderates the reports and can inspect claims, but does not decide ownership.

The application has two parts. The mobile application is written in TypeScript with React Native and Expo and runs on Android phones through Expo Go. It talks to a REST API written in TypeScript with Node.js and Express. Data is stored in a MongoDB Atlas database that uses only two collections, and photographs are stored in Cloudinary. The intended users are the students of the institute and one or more administrators appointed by the institute.

This report describes the problem, the solution, the requirements, the design and the implementation of LostLink as it exists in the project source code at the time of writing.

2. Problem Statement

The informal way in which lost and found belongings are handled on campus creates the following problems:

  1. Dependence on informal communication. Information spreads through WhatsApp groups, notice boards and conversations. Messages are quickly buried under newer ones and only reach the people who happen to be in the same groups.
  2. No central record. There is no single place where a student can check whether their item has been found, or where a finder can announce what they found.
  3. Difficulty locating belongings. Without structured details such as category, location and date, it is hard to search for a specific item or to tell similar items apart.
  4. Difficulty identifying the rightful owner. When a valuable item such as a wallet or phone is found, the finder has no structured way to check that the person asking for it is the real owner. Items can be handed to the wrong person.
  5. No tracking of claims. When several people say an item is theirs, nobody keeps a record of who asked, what they said and what was decided. The claimant also has no way to see the progress of their request.
  6. Difficulty managing many reports. Old posts remain after the item is returned, duplicate posts appear, and nobody can remove misleading or inappropriate posts.

Problem statement: design and develop a mobile application that gives the VJTI campus a single, organised platform for reporting and finding lost and found belongings, with a structured ownership-claim process that lets the finder verify the rightful owner, records the status of every claim, keeps reports accurate, and allows administrators to moderate the content.

3. Proposed Solution

LostLink replaces the scattered channels with a mobile application backed by a central database. The workflow is described below from the point of view of each type of participant.

3.1 A student who lost an item

The student opens Report → Report Lost Item and enters the item name and category. The description and the last seen location are optional for lost items, because the owner may not remember them; the date can be picked from a calendar and a photograph can be attached. The report becomes visible to all students in Browse. The student can search the found reports, contact the person who posted a report through their registered email address, and mark their own lost report as recovered once they have the item back.

3.2 A student who found an item

The finder opens Report Found Item and enters the item name, category, description, the place where it was found and the date, with an optional photograph taken with the camera or chosen from the gallery. The app advises the finder to keep one private detail (for example, initials or contents) out of the description so that it can later be used to check claims.

3.3 A student claiming a found item

A student who recognises a found item taps Claim This Item and writes why the item belongs to them, mentioning identifying details that are not visible in the listing. The claim is stored with status PENDING. The item stays listed while claims are pending, and the claimant can follow the progress of the claim on a timeline in My Activity → My Claims.

3.4 The finder reviewing claims

The finder receives an in-app notification for every new claim and reviews the claims on the item page or in My Activity → Claims Received. Only the finder can approve or reject claims on their item. Approving one claim marks the item as RECOVERED and automatically closes every other pending claim; rejecting a claim (optionally with a reason) leaves the item open for other claims. The claimant is notified of the decision, and the two students arrange the hand-over by email.

3.5 The administrator

The administrator has a separate interface with a dashboard of statistics, a list of all reports and a read-only list of claims. The administrator can view and delete inappropriate or invalid reports (the reporter is notified) but cannot submit, approve or reject claims, so ownership decisions always remain with the finder.

4. Objectives

  1. Develop a centralised lost-and-found platform for the VJTI campus in the form of an Android mobile application.
  2. Make reporting of lost and found items simple, with categories, location, date and an optional photograph from the camera or gallery.
  3. Allow students to browse active listings and to search and filter them by type, category, location, date reported and status.
  4. Provide a structured claim process in which the finder verifies ownership using private identifying details.
  5. Guarantee that only one claim can be approved for an item, and record the status of every claim.
  6. Keep students informed through in-app notifications and a claim progress tracker.
  7. Provide separate, role-based interfaces for students and administrators, with authorisation enforced by the server.
  8. Give administrators tools to view statistics and moderate listings.
  9. Maintain organised and consistent records in a database with a deliberately simple design of two collections.

11. System Architecture

LostLink follows a three-tier client–server architecture (Figure 2). The mobile application never connects to the database or to Cloudinary's upload API directly: every operation goes through the backend, which authenticates the user, validates the request and enforces the business rules.

11.1 Mobile application

Screens are files in mobile/src/app; Expo Router turns the folder structure into navigation. The root layout wraps the app in three providers: AuthProvider (current user and token), ThemeProvider (selected colour theme) and NotificationsProvider (unread count). It then declares the screens inside Stack.Protected groups whose guards depend on the user's role: logged-out users only have Login and Register, students have the (student) tab group and the report and claim forms, and administrators have the (admin) tab group. A screen whose guard is false does not exist for that user, and anyone who reaches it is redirected to an index route that sends them to the correct starting screen. All network calls go through one Axios client in lib/api.ts, which attaches the token, sets timeouts (20 s, or 60 s for uploads) and logs the user out on an HTTP 401 response.

11.2 Backend API

The Express application (backend/app.ts) enables CORS and a JSON body parser limited to 100 KB, mounts five routers and ends with a central error handler that converts validation, upload and duplicate-key errors into short JSON messages without stack traces. Routes that need a logged-in user run requireAuth, which verifies the token and loads the user from the database; administrator routes also run requireAdmin. Image uploads pass through Multer, which keeps the file in memory and accepts only JPG, PNG, WEBP and HEIC files up to 5 MB.

Every response has the same shape: { success, message, data } for success and { success: false, message } for errors, with appropriate HTTP status codes (400 invalid input, 401 not logged in, 403 not allowed, 404 not found, 409 conflict).

11.3 Data and image storage

Mongoose models (User and Item) read and write the two collections in MongoDB Atlas. Photographs are streamed from the server's memory to Cloudinary (folders lostlink/items and lostlink/profiles); the returned secure URL and public id are stored in MongoDB, and the app loads the image directly from the Cloudinary URL. Cloudinary credentials exist only in the backend's environment.

11.4 Authentication and role-based access

Figure 3 shows how a user reaches the correct interface. When the app opens, a saved token is checked with GET /api/auth/me; until this check finishes the app shows a loading screen, so the wrong interface is never displayed. The role in the server's reply decides which tab group is shown. Protection exists at two levels: the app hides and blocks screens by role, and the server independently rejects requests that the user is not allowed to make.

12. Database Design

The database (named LostLink in MongoDB Atlas) contains exactly two collections: users and items. Data that always belongs to one parent document is embedded in it instead of being stored in a separate collection:

  • Claims are an embedded array inside the item they are made on. A claim never exists without its item, the finder always reviews claims item by item, and embedding allows one atomic update to approve a claim, reject the others and change the item's status together.
  • Notifications are an embedded array inside the user they are addressed to, limited to the newest 50.

Relationships between documents use ObjectId references: an item's reportedBy and each claim's claimedBy point to a user, and a notification's relatedItem points to an item (Figure 4).

13. Core Logic

13.1 User authentication

  1. Registration (POST /api/auth/register): the server checks the name length, email format, password length (at least 6) and that the confirmation matches, and rejects an email that is already registered (409). It hashes the password with bcrypt.hash(password, 10), uploads the optional profile photo, and creates the user with the role STUDENT. The role is never read from the request, so nobody can register as an administrator.
  2. Login (POST /api/auth/login): the server finds the user by email, compares the password with bcrypt.compare and returns the same message ("Invalid email or password") whether the email or the password is wrong.
  3. Token generation: on success the server signs a JWT containing the user id with the secret from .env, valid for 7 days, and returns it with the public user fields.
  4. Authorised requests: the app sends Authorization: Bearer <token>. requireAuth verifies the signature and expiry, loads the user and attaches it to the request; requireAdmin additionally requires the role ADMIN.
  5. Administrator accounts are created or updated by npm run seed:admin, which reads the administrator's name, email and password from .env and never creates a duplicate.

13.2 Reporting lost and found items

The report form validates the fields on the phone and then sends a multipart request to POST /api/items containing the fields, the optional photo and a submissionId. The server validates the fields again (Figure 5). Because a mobile connection can drop after the server has saved a report but before the phone receives the answer, report creation is idempotent:

  1. The form creates one random submissionId when it opens and reuses it for every retry. A new id is created only after the server confirms success.
  2. While the request is in progress the whole form is locked by an overlay: fields, category, date and photo buttons are disabled and the user cannot leave the screen.
  3. If a report with the same reporter and submissionId already exists, the server returns that report with HTTP 200 instead of creating another one. If the details differ from the saved ones, it returns 409 so the change is not silently lost.
  4. The unique partial index on (reportedBy, submissionId) is the actual guarantee: if two identical requests arrive at the same moment, the second insert fails with a duplicate-key error, deletes the photo it had uploaded and returns the first report.
  5. If the app receives no reply, it tells the user that the result could not be confirmed, keeps the data and the photo, and explains that submitting again is safe.

New reports have the status ACTIVE and appear in Browse and on Home. The owner of a lost item can later mark it RECOVERED; students can delete their own active reports, while recovered reports are kept as history.

13.3 Browsing and searching

GET /api/items builds a MongoDB filter from optional query parameters: type, category, location (case-insensitive "contains"), q (matches the item name or category), reported (1d, 7d or 30d before now), status (default ACTIVE) and sort (newest or oldest). Text typed by the user is escaped before it is used in a regular expression, and unknown values are rejected with 400. The Browse screen sends the search text 400 ms after the user stops typing and shows active filters as removable chips. Claims are never included in these responses.

13.4 Claim submission

A claim is added with one conditional findOneAndUpdate and $push (Figure 6). The update succeeds only if the item exists, is of type FOUND, has status ACTIVE, was not reported by the claimant, does not already have a claim from this student, and does not already contain the same requestId. Because all these conditions are checked inside a single atomic database operation, two simultaneous requests cannot both add a claim. When the update matches nothing, the server reads the item once more to return a precise reason (for example "This item has already been recovered"), or returns the saved claim if the request is a retry. After a successful claim the reporter receives a CLAIM_RECEIVED notification.

13.5 Claim verification

Only the reporter of the item can view its claims (GET /api/items/:id/claims) and decide them (PATCH /api/items/:id/claims/:claimId). The decision is a single conditional update whose filter requires that the item belongs to the requester, the item is ACTIVE and the chosen claim is PENDING:

  • Approve: the same update sets the item to RECOVERED, the chosen claim to APPROVED and every other pending claim to REJECTED with the reason "The item has already been returned to another claimant." (using MongoDB arrayFilters). Once the item is RECOVERED, no further approval can match the filter, so two claims can never be approved for the same item.
  • Reject: only the chosen claim becomes REJECTED, with the optional reason; the item stays ACTIVE and other students may still claim it.

Figure 7 summarises the resulting status transitions. The claim progress tracker in the app is drawn only from the stored status and timestamps, so it always reflects the database.

13.6 Notifications

Notifications are created only by the backend, immediately after the event they describe, through a helper function notify(). It appends the notification to the user's embedded array with $push and $slice: -50, so only the newest 50 are kept. The events are: a claim received (to the finder), a claim approved, rejected or closed by another approval (to the claimant), and a report removed by an administrator (to the reporter). Because the database updates behind these events can succeed only once, the same notification is never created twice. The app fetches GET /api/users/notifications to show the list and unread count; the count is refreshed every 30 seconds and when the app returns to the foreground. Tapping a notification marks it as read and opens the related item.

13.7 Image upload

  1. The user chooses Camera or Gallery in a bottom sheet. The camera requires permission, which is requested at that moment; if it is refused, the app explains how to enable it.
  2. The picked image is cropped (square for profile photos), compressed to quality 0.7 and checked against the 5 MB limit on the phone.
  3. The image is sent to the backend as part of a multipart request; the phone never holds Cloudinary credentials.
  4. Multer keeps the file in memory and rejects other file types or larger files; the server streams the buffer to Cloudinary and stores the returned URL and public id.
  5. When a profile photo is replaced or removed, or a report is deleted, the old image is deleted from Cloudinary. A photo uploaded for a report that could not be saved is also deleted.

13.8 Administrative moderation

Every administrator route runs requireAuth and requireAdmin. The dashboard statistics are counted by the database (total users, active lost, active found, recovered, reports in the last seven days and pending claims; the pending count uses an aggregation that unwinds the embedded claims). GET /api/admin/items returns all reports, which the app can search and filter; DELETE /api/admin/items/:id removes a report and its photo and notifies the reporter. GET /api/admin/claims lists claims without their private descriptions. There is no administrator route for approving or rejecting claims, and the claim decision route refuses anyone who is not the item's reporter, so ownership decisions remain with the finder.

14. Deployment Details

LostLink has not been deployed to a public production environment. It currently runs in a development setup: the backend runs on a developer's computer, the mobile app is served by the Expo development server and opened in Expo Go on an Android phone or emulator, and the database and image storage use the cloud services MongoDB Atlas and Cloudinary.

14.2 MongoDB Atlas and Cloudinary

  1. Create a free Atlas cluster, add a database user and allow the developer's IP address under Network Access.
  2. Copy the connection string and add the database name (for example …mongodb.net/LostLink?…). The users and items collections and their indexes are created automatically by Mongoose when the server starts.
  3. Create a Cloudinary account and copy the cloud name, API key and API secret into backend/.env. Photos are stored in the folders lostlink/items and lostlink/profiles. Without these values the app still works, but photo uploads are refused.

14.3 Backend setup and execution

cd backend
npm install                # install dependencies
cp .env.example .env       # then fill in the values
npm run seed:admin         # create or update the administrator account
npm run dev                # start the API (port 5000, auto-reload)

A compiled build can be produced with npm run build and started with npm start. GET /api/health reports whether the server is connected to the database.

14.4 Mobile application setup and testing

npm install

On a phone, the Expo Go app scans the QR code shown by the development server; the phone must be on the same network as the computer and able to reach port 5000. On the Android Studio emulator, pressing a in the Expo terminal opens the app. During development the app was checked with tsc --noEmit, ESLint, expo-doctor and an Android bundle export, and tested manually on an Android emulator against the real Atlas and Cloudinary services.

15. Conclusion

LostLink provides the VJTI campus with a single place to report, find and return lost belongings. Students report lost and found items with photographs, search and filter the listings, and claim found items through a structured process in which the finder verifies ownership. The claim workflow is implemented with atomic database updates, so an item can never be given to two claimants, and report and claim submissions are protected against duplicates caused by repeated taps or lost connections. Notifications and a claim progress tracker keep students informed, while administrators moderate the content from a separate interface without deciding ownership.

The project combines a React Native (Expo) mobile application, a Node.js/Express API, MongoDB Atlas and Cloudinary, using a deliberately simple data model of two collections with embedded claims and notifications. The main remaining steps towards real use on campus are deployment of the backend to a public server, an installable Android build and push notifications.
